# Mathematics Through Representations

## Map design

- Sequence observable mathematical capabilities and their prerequisites, not chapter-name topic buckets.
- Match module and lesson count to the confirmed goal. A single named theorem, identity, or proof is one module with one (or few) lessons; do not expand it into a textbook chapter sequence.
- Keep proof construction, cases, and verification inside one lesson (a formal derivation section). Do not open a lesson per lemma step.
- Revisit important ideas across symbolic, numerical, verbal, tabular, and graphical representations.
- Progress from interpreting a representation to constructing one, connecting representations, solving, justifying, and transferring.
- Separate procedural fluency from conceptual understanding and mathematical justification; plan practice for each capability the goal promises.

## Unit teaching

- Start with a precise question, pattern, or problem that gives the new idea a mathematical purpose.
- Move through meaning → representation → worked reasoning → learner attempt → comparison or variation → independent problem.
- Show the transformations that carry the reasoning. Name the property, theorem, invariant, or assumption that licenses each important step.
- Use exact values before approximations unless estimation is the objective. State domains, units, constraints, boundary cases, and possible extraneous solutions when relevant.
- Connect formulas to what their terms and changes mean. Ask the learner to predict a sign, shape, direction, scale, bound, or trend before calculating when useful.
- Treat visual representation as a normal part of mathematics teaching, more often than in other domains. Proactively look for a graph or mathematical scene whenever position, shape, direction, variation, accumulation, transformation, distribution, or multiple representations carry the core insight.
- Use `math_scene` for functions, coordinate geometry, vectors, transformations, intersections, parametric or polar curves, and surfaces. Use `chart` for supplied or computed tabular, statistical, or empirical data. Use `svg_slot` for conceptual relationships, dependencies, or processes—not as a coordinate plot.
- Plan multiple focused mathematical scenes when distinct core relationships each need inspection; do not compress unrelated graphs into one crowded display. Keep prose primary when a visual would add no mathematical information.
- Introduce every visual with a prediction or observation task and follow it with an interpretation. Label axes, scales, units, important points, parameters, and compared objects, and ensure the visual agrees with the equations and stated domain.

## Scenario theater

- Put mathematics inside a credible modeling, estimation, optimization, risk, measurement, or decision problem with incomplete information and defensible alternatives.
- Make choices select assumptions, representations, quantities, bounds, strategies, or interpretations—not disguised arithmetic answers.
- Let consequences follow from the mathematical model, including sensitivity to assumptions, uncertainty, scale, feasibility, and error.
- Keep symbolic manipulation and proof in lesson practice; theater demonstrates mathematical judgment but does not by itself prove calculation fluency.

## Practice and evidence

- Use recognition only for recall or interpretation. Require calculation, construction, explanation, proof, or model choice when the target promises those capabilities.
- Vary surface details and representation while preserving the underlying structure; use a meaningfully different context or representation for transfer.
- Diagnose errors by locating the first invalid assumption, representation, transformation, computation, or interpretation. Preserve valid work, explain the governing idea, and ask for a targeted retry.
- Require learners to check results with estimation, substitution, inverse operations, units, boundary behavior, a second representation, or a counterexample as appropriate.
- Treat watching a derivation or manipulating an already-correct graph as exposure, not demonstrated mastery.

## Adaptation

- Give beginners concrete quantities, annotated diagrams, partially completed representations, and one decision per step; fade these supports as accuracy and explanation improve.
- Give experienced learners less routine problems, competing methods, proof obligations, modeling assumptions, edge cases, and opportunities to generalize.
- If prerequisite gaps block progress, teach the smallest missing idea in context and return to the original problem rather than silently lowering the target.
